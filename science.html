The Science: Three Walls Blocking Blue

Bandgap, crystal quality and the p-type problem

Home | About | The Science

Wall 1: A Material With a Wide Bandgap

An LED's colour depends on the bandgap of its semiconductor. Blue light (about 450 nm) needs a bandgap of roughly 2.7 eV. Silicon and gallium arsenide are far too narrow. Candidates were GaN (3.4 eV), SiC and ZnSe.

Semiconductor comparison
Material Bandgap Colour Problem
GaAsP about 1.9 eV Red None, easy to make
SiC about 2.9 eV Dim blue Indirect bandgap, very weak light
ZnSe about 2.7 eV Blue-green Short lifetime, defects spread
GaN about 3.4 eV UV (blue with In added) Hard crystals, hard p-type

Wall 2: Growing Good Crystals

GaN has no cheap matching substrate. Sapphire was used, but its atoms do not line up with GaN, so the crystal came out rough and full of defects.

Breakthrough: Amano and Akasaki (1986) added a thin aluminium nitride (AlN) buffer layer. Nakamura (1991) used a low-temperature GaN buffer layer. Both gave smooth, high-quality crystals.

Wall 3: The p-type Problem

An LED needs both n-type and p-type layers. n-type GaN was easy, but p-type GaN seemed impossible. When magnesium (the p-type dopant) was added, hydrogen atoms bonded to it and switched it off made it inactive.

  1. 1989: Akasaki and Amano used an electron beam to activate magnesium (first p-type GaN).
  2. 1992: Nakamura showed that simple heating (thermal annealing) drives out the hydrogen.
  3. 1993: Nakamura built the first bright blue LED using an InGaN junction.

Conclusion

The blue LED was not physically impossible. It was a stack of problems (material, crystals and p-type doping) that each looked unsolvable until a few stubborn researchers solved them one by one.
